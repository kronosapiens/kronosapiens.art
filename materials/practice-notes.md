# Notes for the artist statement discussion

These are connections Daniel identified or recognized while discussing the five works.
The quotations are his words from those conversations.

## Rules and beauty

In *Infinite Knot* the form was already known and loved.
The measured grid let Daniel make it again at the scale of a particular wall or door.
In *Identity Matrix* the figures came by chance, and the rule came afterward.
The pleasure there was in being able to “completely cover all of the ground of combinations in a single tight composition.”
That interest returned when he worked out how to generate a different map for each player in *The Lost Temple Adventure*.

The construction matters to him as craft and as something a critic can discover.
Someone who simply enjoys looking at the work and feels calm has had an experience he values in its own right.

Asked which work he would show beside *Space Prince*, Daniel chose *Identity Matrix*.
He sees the mural’s way of working with color and form in both the game’s appearance and its mechanics.
He wants the chart to have the finish of jewelry and still be legible during play.
His test for the results is sensory.

> Sometimes I feel like almost like a medieval astronomer.
> I'm using all of these systems and frameworks to try to navigate this huge creative space.
> And then at the end, you just have to use your eyes and your ears and your heart and just say, does this feel good?
> Does this feel familiar yet different?

He also described looking for that feeling as “its own kind of spiritual spaceship journey”.

The score is another example he might use in writing about the work.
He cited Gustav Holst when describing his wish to give each planet a musical character.
The main theme is in D, and the seven planets have seven different modes.
Astra generated the tracks using that framework and the planetary symbolism.
They went through several rounds of sketches and changed some samples after hearing them.
Daniel would play the main theme first and let a listener move among all seven planets.
He suggested Mars as an easy comparison because its aggression is audible right away.

## Making something for a place

In Berkeley the house murals offered a way to contribute to the life of the co-op.
In Yaffo friends were making stencil art, and Daniel tried it on the rooftop he shared with his roommates.
In the games, the invitation could run the other way.
A player asked to take their vow for a digital badge at a medieval shrine in Kyoto, and the Devconnect flyers drew players across a conference venue to solve puzzles together.

One Halloween as a Berkeley undergraduate, Daniel joined a public zombie game in San Francisco with two housemates.
Players traveled from the ferry terminal toward Civic Center on foot or by public transit.
Each human caught became another zombie, so the same simple rule made the trip more difficult as the night went on.
What he remembers is the exhilaration of moving through the streets back-to-back with his friends.
The city gave them situations the organizers could never have designed.

> Maybe another way to put it is if anywhere you go there might be something you can stumble across, then everywhere you go the entire world becomes a site of potential adventure.
> Even where the game is not actually present.

## Effort, attachment, and the save file

“Where does that energy go?” was Daniel’s question about the hours people spend in games.
Proof of work offered a model for a digital trace of effort.
Generative art showed how an image could have its own provenance, and a save file already held a record of what someone had actually done.
He recalls a friend having to start *Baldur’s Gate 3* over when an update corrupted a save after roughly eighty hours.

Matt DesLauriers’s *Meridian* was a particular influence because of the beauty its compact program could produce.
Tyler Hobbs’s writing on generative art was another influence.
Erick Calderon’s work suggested how an image could belong to a series and still feel particular to the person who received it.
Daniel’s question for the games was whether playing could give such an image emotional value. [Matt DesLauriers on *Meridian*](https://meridian.mattdesl.com/)

The sword rack in *Ronin’s Pact* records trials completed.
The map in *The Lost Temple Adventure* records a journey someone made.
In *Space Prince*, Daniel wants the player to recognize growth in how they handled an encounter and to have something they can point out to someone else.

## Attention

Painting an endless knot felt meditative once the lines were in place.
Hesse’s *Siddhartha* informs Daniel’s ambition for a player in *Space Prince* to respond to a situation with “skill and grace and attunement.”
He recognized the connection between those experiences in hindsight during the conversation.
The kind of play he describes remains an aspiration for the game in progress.

For Daniel, playing with your own birth chart means you could recognize a planetary relationship from experience if you later had the chart read.
His first teaching goal is for a player to get to know the planets as characters.

> I think just having a player think, I have a Sun, I have a Moon, I have a Mars, that's what my chart is about.

He described growing attached to the idea of himself as an Aquarian creative when he was younger.
These days he finds it more useful to think about his different drives and to notice when acting on one has been helpful or careless toward another person.
He hopes a player will be able to see growth in how they respond to an encounter and to point it out to somebody else.
Every run leaves a star, including the runs in which the player would now make different choices.

> But I think even a run that ended very poorly with a dim star is a memory of that experience.
