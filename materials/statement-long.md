# Artist statement

Looking over a page of his own doodles, Daniel Kronovet saw a face in profile, another looking straight at him, and what might have been a person holding balloons.
He made stencils of the three figures and took three cans of spray paint up to the rooftop he shared with his roommates in Yaffo.
In the finished mural, *Identity Matrix*, each figure appears in each possible pair of colors exactly once.
Kronovet takes pleasure in how neatly the nine combinations fit.
He would be just as happy for someone to linger over the way the teal looks beside the fuchsia.

The work Kronovet would put beside that mural is *Space Prince*, the astrology game he is developing.
He wants its birth chart to have the finish of a piece of jewelry and still be easy to read during play.
Its seven planets are characters a player can get to know by ear.
The score has a main theme in D and a different musical mode for each planet.
An AI called Astra generated the tracks from those modes and the planets’ symbolism.
Some of the sounds it chose grated on Kronovet, so he and Astra changed the samples.
He likens working with these systems to being a medieval astronomer, finding his way among more possibilities than he could otherwise make sense of.
Once he has something to look at or listen to, his question is simpler.
“Does this feel familiar yet different?”

Kronovet learned to paint murals in the Berkeley student co-ops, where contributing a painting was part of the life of a house.
For *Infinite Knot*, his first mural, he chose an out-of-the-way stairwell and drew a Tibetan Buddhist endless knot on a measured grid.
By the time he picked up a brush, he could settle into the meditative work of following the lines.
Around the same time, he and two friends spent Halloween running back-to-back through San Francisco in a public game of humans and zombies.
The organizers had given them a few rules and an entire city to play in.
What stayed with him was how it felt to be on those streets when an encounter might be waiting around any corner.

Years later, at a conference in Buenos Aires, he put up flyers asking, “Can you find the lost temple?”
Players followed clues to puzzles around the venue and played games on their phones, filling in a digital map as they went.
Kronovet had been thinking about the hours people spend playing games and what becomes of that effort.
In *Ronin’s Pact*, he had experimented with making the save file itself an artwork players could keep, a sword rack that gained a sword with each completed trial.
In *The Lost Temple Adventure*, the map was the save file.
Each player had a route of their own, and Kronovet hoped that looking at it later might bring back the people they had spent the afternoon with.

In *Space Prince*, a player can begin with the chart of the sky at the time and place of their birth.
Kronovet once liked to think of himself as an Aquarian creative.
As he has grown older, the planets have given him a way to think about the different drives he recognizes in himself and how acting on them affects other people.
He wants a player to get a feel for their own Mars or Moon through the choices they make in an encounter.
Each run leaves a star in the player’s sky.
A bright one gives the player something to strive for.
Kronovet hopes even a dim one might be worth pointing out to someone months later, when the player remembers a choice they would make differently now.
