# Does this feel familiar yet different?

Daniel Kronovet on painting a knot in a Berkeley stairwell, running a scavenger hunt in Buenos Aires, and playing a game as yourself.

*The questions from Codex have been shortened, and Daniel’s answers have been lightly edited for grammar and length.*
*[Read the verbatim conversation.](portfolio-conversation.md)*

## Infinite Knot

**Codex** — Take me back to the yellow door in 2009.
What drew you to that particular knot, and how did you come to paint it there?

**Daniel Kronovet** — I was an undergraduate at UC Berkeley, living in the student housing co-ops.
I was very exposed to Northern Californian spirituality, with all of the Buddhist influences, and I became really enamored with the Tibetan Buddhist endless knot.
I found it so beautiful and pleasing.
I loved the visual effect of the patterning over and under, creating this eternal knot effect.

In the Berkeley co-ops there was a practice of students painting murals as part of living in the house.
Most of the houses were known for these elaborate, beautiful murals on the walls, and contributing a mural was a way to contribute to the cultural life of the group.
I lived in three houses, and in every house I lived in for at least a semester, I would do one of these knots.
The image I’m showing here was the first one I ever did, in a house called Casa Zimbabwe, which was 124 students.
I’d never done one before and I didn’t want to take up prime visible real estate in the house.
So I chose a small, out-of-the-way stairwell and gave it a shot.

It began with a sketch.
I’d taken some architecture courses, so the idea of starting with a draft made sense to me.
I would measure the space.
The knot is a geometric shape, so it’s a very mathematical construction.
You arrange the grid of dots exactly uniformly in the space, then follow a specific line pattern of over-under to create the outline.
Once the outline is in place, you go from a ruler and pencil to a paintbrush.
At that point it’s really just coloring inside the lines.

Choosing a good color scheme is an aesthetic choice.
In this one I believe it was a greenish blue.
In others I would do a greenish blue again, then later on a red and gold.
The actual painting takes a little care and thought, but no deep inspiration.
I found that executing the piece was really more meditation, appropriately for the subject matter.
It was a meditative, almost spiritual practice of executing this shape in this site-specific way, in a way that I felt added to the space I was living in.
I’ve tried to find photos of the other ones, but it’s been surprisingly hard.
This was 16 years ago and my mobile phones are deep in storage.
I’d have to go rummaging through them to get the photos out.

## Identity Matrix

**Codex** — Where did the signs in *Identity Matrix* come from?
How did you get the title?

**Daniel** — *Identity Matrix* came a lot later.
I was living in Yaffo, in Israel/Palestine, in a community of creative people.
Everyone was making art.
I’d finished graduate school and was earlyish career, working remotely for a British crypto company.
I wanted to explore myself creatively in this beautiful environment.

I’d gotten a sketchbook and just started drawing lines, doodling almost in a stream of consciousness.
I looked at one of the sheets of my chicken scratch and noticed that a few of the doodles looked like interesting shapes.
What’s the word for when you see faces in objects?
I saw what looked to me like a face in profile, just a nose and a mouth.
Then I saw a face in portrait, straight on, just eyes and a mouth and a nose.
And then an abstracted person, maybe holding some balloons, the three stars.
That was, I suppose, a person at a distance.

I’d gotten some spray paint because a lot of my friends were doing street art, and stenciling was something I wanted to try.
There was a very vibrant street art stencil culture in the Yaffo area.
As in the co-ops, that was how people contributed to the shared culture of the group.
When I got the paint I thought, how many colors do I need?
Let me think of some nice colors I can recombine effectively.
I’m not a color theorist, but let’s start with some basic primaries.
I ended up with an orange, a fuchsia, and a pleasant teal, almost a rotation down the color wheel from red, yellow, blue.
They still existed in a kind of three-part relationship.

So I have three images and three colors.
How can I systematize this into a complete expression of some kind of concept?
What if I chose two of the three colors for every shape?
For one image it would be the teal and the fuchsia, for one it would be the orange and the teal.
I realized I could have a three-by-three grid where, almost Sudoku-style, every shape appeared once in every row and once in every column.
Every shape was executed in every possible combination of two of the three colors.
There were three or six possible combinations, I actually forget.
The point was that every color combination would appear once in every row and column.
You had this kind of perfect matrix where every combination of color and form was expressed once.
I found that so pleasant as an abstract aesthetic to contemplate, being able to completely cover all the ground of combinations in a single tight composition.

I did it one day on the wall of the rooftop I shared with my roommates.
It was three guys with a shared rooftop space.
It was very fun.
I’d planned out where every image went and what every color combination was going to look like.
Then it was a pretty quick, tight execution.
You put the stencil up, try to spray without getting too much on the wall around it so the lines and colors look tight, and put the mural up.

The name came at the end.
I’d studied linear algebra in graduate school and before.
An identity matrix is a grid of numbers that, when you multiply another matrix by it, gives you the matrix you started with.
It’s very bread and butter, but I thought it was a pleasant name, to lift a bit of linear algebra branding for this work.
The images are ostensibly three views of a person, so it was a matrix about a person.
I thought the name *Identity Matrix* was very cute.

## Ronin’s Pact

**Codex** — What was the occasion for *Ronin’s Pact*, and where did the sword rack come from?

**Daniel** — *Ronin’s Pact* was really a sketch, more a proof of concept of some ideas than something meant to be a complete work in itself.
By that time I’d accumulated a lot of influences.
I’d been inspired by *Pokémon Go* and the idea of digital technology creating an enchanted, augmented reality above and beyond what the world itself could offer.
The Jejune Institute in San Francisco was an influence.
The idea of a scavenger hunt, a citywide adventure you could go on, was very exciting.

Because I’d been involved in Web3 and digital governance design, I was interested in how you could make a scavenger hunt that didn’t require a huge amount of staff or operational complexity, that could be self-run or self-governed.
Could scanning a QR code give reliable proof that you’d solved a puzzle and still be resistant to someone just posting the QR code online?
I spent a long time noodling over those problems without really getting anywhere.

I had this idea that if someone finished the scavenger hunt they would earn a badge, a medallion.
The Explorers Club in New York was an inspiration there.
If you finished a real-world adventure, you could earn the right to access this very cool members’ space.
In different cities there would be bars with different themes.
The one I would do would be Andromeda, with a Hellenistic Greek theme.
Finishing the adventure would have been a challenging thing to do, and it would have meant something.
You’d feel like being with your fellow adventurers in this physical location was where you wanted to be.
Going on adventures with your friends, experiencing your city in a more enchanted, magical way, then actually getting access via this NFT medallion to a space you could go back to again and again to meet people, all in a self-administered way, was very exciting to me.

I was working at Cartridge, which made infrastructure for fully onchain games, and helping organize their hackathons.
I was in this mix of digital artists and onchain game makers exploring the idea of a video game session as a token.
The session itself was an NFT you could use and transfer, a new kind of protocol for creating interactive game experiences.
The Loot NFT project was very inspiring to that group.
I’d also been inspired by Art Blocks, Erick Calderon, and Tyler Hobbs and their writing about generative art.
The idea of a person forming a unique relationship with a digital collectible interested me, and the scavenger hunt being almost a type of proof of work, of achievement.

The framework the hackathon was based on was called Dojo, so I already had a Japanese samurai theme from the company branding.
That wasn’t really my choice.
I thought it would be interesting to develop this fully onchain, dynamic SVG NFT.
Anyone could mint one, and it would be their personal badge or medallion and also the “save file” of their adventure.
As they did simple activities, the token itself would change as they unlocked different swords.
Through gameplay they could craft a badge that reflected a certain level of achievement.
In the context of the hackathon, if they finished the Pact they could get some benefit, or maybe there was a Discord role they could earn.
That’s the exciting thing about the NFT save file.
It becomes an interface that can connect to other experiences, the way Loot was the basis for other digital experiences.

Then it became, what can I implement in probably a week or two that makes sense in the context of this activation and lets me kick the tires on these ideas?
I’d brought all these ideas, and now I could get paid to explore them through my normal day job.
I thought that was a very nice opportunity.

I worked with my teammate, our designer, to develop the visual assets.
I coded up the Cairo NFT contract and the client myself.
People really enjoyed it.
I think there’s a screenshot of someone in Discord asking if they could go up to some shrine in Kyoto to take their vow.
I thought that was very cute.
That was exactly what I was going for, the idea that this digital layer is adding something to the physical world we’re already living in.

## The Lost Temple Adventure

**Codex** — What could you try in *The Lost Temple Adventure* that you’d only sketched in *Ronin’s Pact*?
What do you remember from Devconnect?

**Daniel** — *The Lost Temple Adventure* was basically the same idea pushed further.
I was still working at Cartridge, maybe a month or two after *Ronin’s Pact*.
We’d been asked to contribute something to Devconnect, the Ethereum conference, and I thought, why don’t we pitch a scavenger hunt?

I’d done a couple of these hunts with friends in the Ethereum ecosystem.
Some of my teammates at Colony, my first job in crypto, were very into digital puzzles.
It was a wonderful team.
Griffin and Aaron and their friends developed a group that did elaborate scavenger hunts at crypto conferences.
I reached out to see if they were coming to Devconnect, and they said they probably weren’t going to do anything that year.
I asked if they’d mind if I pitched something through Cartridge, and they said, sure, go for it.
I reached out to one of the Ethereum organizers and said I’d done a lot of work with Daedalus Industries and Social Dist0rtion Protocol and would love to do a scavenger hunt.
They were really open to the idea.
The treasure hunt was kind of the other group’s brand, so we’d call this one the adventure.

*Ronin’s Pact* was very single-player, with no real physical components.
With *The Lost Temple Adventure*, I could integrate physical puzzles into a digital game where the save file, the collectible adventure map, was an intimate part of the experience.
Can you create an experience where a player feels like they’re accruing emotional value to a digital collectible as a side effect of gameplay?
That’s the save file as art object idea.
It’s almost proof-of-work inspired.
People spend dozens, sometimes a hundred or more hours in these digital worlds.
Where does that energy go?
It goes into the save file, a piece of data we treat so casually that’s really the only mark, the record in the physical world of that experience.
Elevating that and centering it as part of the game struck me as so cool.

An adventure map as the NFT made tons of sense to me.
I thought generating the route procedurally for each account was a clever little way to push the customization.
This was the Erick Calderon “1 of 1 of X” idea.
Every map should feel like someone’s personal adventure.
Making that feasible onchain was an interesting engineering exercise, similar to *Identity Matrix*.
How do you get the implementation to be efficient and complete while still providing the necessary variety?
That was the generative art conversation I was trying to enter into.

Then, how do I make this a fun game?
What’s the storytelling that gives this digital apparatus some emotional valence?
The lost temple was such a classic, pulpy narrative frame.
I thought, there’s enough going on here, let’s stick to the basics.
There’s really only a gesture toward a story.
There’s a lost temple.
We have to go find it.
There’s nothing much there if you look closely, but there’s enough to give the player a sense of emotional orientation.

Because I was doing this through Cartridge, I had to think about the business strategy.
We had a gaming-focused web wallet, the Controller, that we wanted to elevate.
People in our ecosystem were developing the idea of the game as a save file and the embeddable game standard.
We wanted people playing those games.
I could sell this to my teammates because people would come into the Starknet gaming ecosystem and play our games, and at the same time they’d go around the venue doing puzzles.
I got permission from the organizers to set up the physical puzzles.
Running the game at the festival took half my time, which was fine.
The Super Nintendo ended up getting stolen, which is too bad, but that was my own fault.

It ended up being six stages, three of them onchain.
You had to go on your phone, play a round of an onchain game, and win.
For the three physical puzzles you’d get a clue to the location and go around the venue trying to find the puzzle.
Solving it gave you a codeword you’d enter into the app to unlock the next stage.

To my discredit, the puzzles were generally too difficult.
It’s very easy to design a puzzle that we think is simple because we know what we’re thinking.
Someone else comes into it thinking something completely different.
Unless you’ve done playtesting, even a puzzle you think is simple can be extremely difficult.

The first puzzle was a ’90s living room.
I had an original Super Nintendo, some games, and back issues of *Nintendo Power*.
I’d fantasized about finding an old TV in Buenos Aires and setting up a working Super Nintendo.
That didn’t happen, so it was the Super Nintendo as a sculptural object, plus the magazines as almost an aesthetic.
The puzzle was called *Cracking the Code*, and the games in the Nintendo were Konami games.
I thought people would see the Super Nintendo and *Nintendo Power*, know the puzzle was called *Cracking the Code*, see that the games were Konami games, and immediately understand that the solution was Konami, the Konami Code.
That was what I thought, very *Ready Player One*.
In practice, no one had any idea what I was talking about.
Not a single person got the clue unless I helped them.

A friend had given me the idea of adding a Telegram help link to the game client.
That saved the whole game because I could give people clues when they got stuck and they could continue playing.
If that hadn’t been in place, no one would have finished.
I’d made little QR code flyers asking, “Can you find the lost temple?” and put them up all over the venue with permission from the organizers.
I think we had about a hundred people mint a map and start the game.
Maybe twenty finished one puzzle, and I think four or five finished the whole adventure.
The people who got into it really enjoyed it.
I have photos of people doing the puzzles together and having a good time.
I felt it was a very successful activation.
It let me push these ideas I’d had for so long much further, get another rep in, and turn them into something people could experience and emotionally relate to.

## Space Prince

**Codex** — How did *Space Prince* begin?
When did a birth chart strike you as something you could play?

**Daniel** — *Space Prince* came kind of out of nowhere, a few months after *The Lost Temple Adventure*.
I’d been immersed in the Starknet gaming community and influenced by a game called *Loot Survivor 2* that Provable Games made.
I thought it was phenomenal.
It was a fully onchain roguelike where you were an adventurer, leveling up your stats and fighting monsters, and it was very difficult.
The designers had created a fairly rich game inside a highly constrained blockchain computing environment, using rock-paper-scissors mechanics and different types of gear, monsters, and weapons.
Rock-paper-scissors is a workhorse of onchain gaming.
People still play the classic.
Even though it’s so mechanistically simple, there’s enough depth to make it a cool game.
I thought it was interesting that onchain gaming was returning to mechanistic fundamentals to make the games feasible.

*Loot Survivor* had an NFT collection called the Beast Collection, ordinary NFTs that had stats.
The idea was that the beasts would live inside the game.
You’d pay fifty cents, a dollar, insert coin to play a run, a very classic arcade model.
If you met and defeated a powerful beast in the dungeon, you’d collect that token.
You were going into a digital dungeon to search for “real” treasure in the form of these scarce digital collectibles.
They were inspired by the Loot project, which was one of the big influences at the time, alongside the generative art conversation happening in parallel.

I was at an opening at Nguyen Wahed Gallery on the Lower East Side in January of ’26.
I ran into Haiver, the director of Artist Commons, who I’d gotten to know through digital art events in New York City.
We have a friendly rapport.
It was a Friday, and he said he was starting a program on Monday, they had a couple of extra seats, and would I be interested?
I thought, well, yes.
I was already exploring in this direction through work, and I felt I had the capacity that year to take on a creative project and grow artistically.
He said, “What would you do?”
I thought, what *would* I do?
The idea for astrology as the mechanics came out of nowhere, fully formed from the head of Zeus.

I was familiar with astrology and appreciated it as a symbolic system.
I had this idea that the birth chart would be the save file and the character sheet, kind of the Erick Calderon “one of one of X” idea again.
You’d develop gameplay using those workhorse rock-paper-scissors-type mechanics and let it emerge out of a series of constraints.
I had no idea where it was going to go or what the game would look like.
I spent the whole next day, Saturday, riffing with ChatGPT on the concept.
I have that chat, actually.
Here’s my initial prompt.

> Space Prince is an onchain TCG in which players mint characters based on astrological charts. Each character is a unique lat / long / timestamp corresponding to an actual birth location. An onchain calculation of the astrological chart becomes the character stats, with 12 astrological signs and 4 elements driving the stats. Combat is a rock-paper-scissors or five-element cycle among the various signs. Combat details and progression are TBD but will likely involve roguelike battling and metaprogression. One very nice implication of this design is that people can play as themselves, as famous historical figures, etc.

I’ve been working on it for ten and a half months now.
It’s mid-November, and the game’s come a long way.
It’s a work in progress.
The idea was that through playing a well-designed game, one that worked as a game, people could experience this more abstract symbolic system of astrology, which I have a lot of respect for.
I’d like it to become almost a teaching tool, with enough of an isomorphism between the mechanics and the astrology that you really do learn astrology through playing.

## Where the time goes

**Codex** — Say someone sees the person with balloons in *Identity Matrix* and never notices how the grid works.
Would that feel complete to you?

**Daniel** — It’s not essential to me that the viewer appreciate the intricate construction of the work for it to be good.
I’d be very happy if the viewer thought it was pretty and pleasant and wanted to spend time with it, and it made them feel calm.
I’d want an art critic, or someone looking at it with a critical eye, to appreciate the construction as part of the artistry and craft.
If someone discovers the intricacies, that’s very pleasant, but I don’t need every viewer to notice them right away.

**Codex** — Is there a particular save file or game character from your own life that made you think about where those hours go?

**Daniel** — That idea came out of the interaction of other ideas I was being exposed to.
There was the crypto idea of proof of work, how you can capture effort in a digital artifact, and the generative artworks I was seeing in the Art Blocks community.
I remember Matt DesLauriers’s *Meridian* was very inspiring to me, how beautiful the images could be coming out of such a small piece of code.
I was interested in creating images onchain as provenanced artworks, and in the save file as a place where value goes.
I’ve played many games and had many save files.
I never thought about them in that way until I started thinking about these other things.
The ideas started to interlock in my mind in a way that led to this pretty clear idea.

There’s a story.
A friend of mine, Ben, was playing *Baldur’s Gate 3*.
He’s a huge gamer and he’d put maybe eighty hours into it.
We were chatting and he joked about how a system update had corrupted the save file and he had to start over.
He wasn’t distraught, but he was definitely annoyed.
I thought that was funny, and I thought, huh, that seems wrong.
The fact that a system update could glitch your save file seems pretty not okay.

Those three ideas came together.
There could be a digital trace of actual investment and effort, a digital artifact with an aesthetic quality as an artwork.
Gameplay could be that kind of proof of work where, instead of a blockchain hash, you get data in a save file that could attain a visual quality the player feels connected to emotionally.

**Codex** — When a player looks back at a star from a run of *Space Prince*, what do you hope they remember?

**Daniel** — My highest aspiration would be for them to feel that the time they spent with the game was meaningful, that they had actually grown through the experience, and the star was an objective reflection of that growth.
Something they could point to, and point others to, and say, “This shows who I am.”

**Codex** — What does growth mean to you here?

**Daniel** — One of the big influences on how I think about the gameplay is Hermann Hesse’s novel *Siddhartha*.
The main character begins life as this sort of prince who leaves the privileged world he knew to go on a journey of enlightenment.
First he becomes an ascetic monk, then a worldly man in the city, then he lives by a river as a boatman.
None of those experiences is the enlightenment he seeks.
He’s always looking for it and never quite finds it, but through this journey of development and experience he comes to attain an enlightened state.

I think about my own life and my own development.
How do I try to respond to situations with more skill and grace?
How do I respond, not react?
How do I attune to my impact on others?
My ambition for the game, which I think has yet to be achieved, is that gameplay feels a lot like that.
You generate Light, the game’s score, by responding to these situations with skill and grace and attunement.
Light, enlightenment.
Someone could point to a star and think, yeah, I really navigated that situation well.

**Codex** — Does the attention you hope a player brings to an encounter feel related to the attention you gave the knot?

**Daniel** — There’s probably some truth to that, even though it wasn’t something I was thinking about explicitly.
There’s the calm, meditative state for me making the knots, and for a player thinking about how they respond to their situations.
For someone to attain a meditative and attentive state would be a goal.
I think that’s a line you could draw.

## A city as a game board

**Codex** — What appeals to you about making something people can stumble across in the course of their day?

**Daniel** — It links to the idea of the augmented reality game and of enchanting the environment with magic.
Things you discover along your way that are portals to a larger adventure are a core part of the worldbuilding or aesthetic I’m going for.
Maybe another way to put it is, if anywhere you go there might be something you can stumble across, then everywhere you go the entire world becomes a site of potential adventure.
Even where the game is not actually present.

**Codex** — Can you remember being on the receiving end of that feeling?

**Daniel** — One Halloween when I was an undergraduate, some housemates and I went to San Francisco for an elaborate public zombie game.
You started off human and there were some zombies, and you had to go from the ferry terminal all the way to Civic Center on foot or public transit.
You were in public space, and the zombies would try to catch the humans and turn them into zombies.
As the game progressed there were more zombies and fewer humans.
Once you were a zombie, the fun was trying to catch humans.
While you were human, the fun was evading the zombies.
The game naturally got harder as the night progressed.

I went with two of my friends, and I remember walking through the streets hyper-alert, running back-to-back, fleeing from the zombies.
The exhilaration I felt was like almost nothing else I’ve experienced.
I just remembered how that felt.

**Codex** — What did playing in a real city add?

**Daniel** — On a game course every detail would have been added by the organizers, so it would have been more flat and sparse.
In a city the entire city becomes a game board full of depth and nuance and complexity that no designer could create, because it’s an actual place.
Otherwise you’re playing laser tag, which is fun.
I think this is more interesting.

## Playing as yourself

**Codex** — What does it mean to play *Space Prince* with the chart from the moment you were born?

**Daniel** — “Playing as yourself” in the context of an astrology game is very important to me.
Your learning of the game, your development of intuition for how to act, is grounded directly in your actual chart.
If you ever got your chart read, or looked into it in an astrology context, you’d understand more intuitively what those dynamics are expressing, maybe even more than if someone read your chart to you.
Letting people experience their chart through gameplay adds so much to having a flat chart interpreted by an astrologer.
It gives the game meaning outside of the game because it changes the way you understand yourself.
A generic chart would lose a lot of that impact.

**Codex** — What part of a chart would you most like a player to understand intuitively?

**Daniel** — A good basic goal is to give the player a feeling for all of their planets, almost as characters.
Most people know about their Sun sign and their Moon sign and their rising, but rarely think about the Sun and Moon part of that sentence.
They think, I’m an Aquarius or I’m a Scorpio.
Your chart is about your planets.
That’s who you are.
The signs are just the planets’ positions.
Shifting the focus to the planets as the bulk of who you are would be a good, attainable early learning goal, before aspects and interactions and all those things.
Just having a player think, I have a Sun, I have a Moon, I have a Mars.
That’s what my chart is about.

**Codex** — What does that shift from “I am” to “I have” mean to you personally?

**Daniel** — As I’ve gotten older and hopefully more mature, I’ve learned more about taking accountability for my actions and being aware of my impact on others.
It’s kind of nice to shift from “I am an Aquarius,” this positive mythic identity of an Aquarian creative that’s just abstract in the world, to “I’m made of planets and drives.”
Some of them motivate me this way and that way.
Sometimes I act in a way that’s beneficial to others, and sometimes in a way that’s destructive or careless.
Maintaining awareness of that over some abstract ideal identity is a nice way to symbolize and reflect personal growth and self-understanding.

**Codex** — Could you value a star because it reminded you of a time you’d been careless?

**Daniel** — Certainly, why not?
Every run puts a star in the sky.
Higher Light, a brighter star, gives people something to strive for.
Even a run that ended very poorly, with a dim star, is a memory of that experience.

## Color, form, and sound

**Codex** — Which of your other works would you show someone alongside *Space Prince*?

**Daniel** — I’d show *Identity Matrix*.
There’s the same curiosity about systemization of color and form and the emergent aesthetic beauty that can come out of that.
I tried to apply those same ideas to the visual design of *Space Prince* as well as to the mechanistic design.
A lot of the same underlying interests are being re-expressed in this more complex form.

**Codex** — Where does that thinking show up in the design of the chart?

**Daniel** — One of the big tensions was wanting ornate visual embellishments to give it a feeling of refinement, almost jewelry, while wanting it to be functional as a game board.
The information you need to play has to be very legible.
Those two things pushed in opposite directions, and working through that has led to the form of the chart as it is today.
I think it’s in pretty decent shape.
Visually and sound-wise, the game is going very well.
It’s come together at a level of quality I’d accept, modulo a few open questions around achievement design and so on.

**Codex** — If you could put headphones on me for ten seconds of *Space Prince*, what would you play?

**Daniel** — The main theme is just so good.
Similar to the visuals, the sound design revolved around creating a system out of musical fundamentals.
Basically, most video game music is in a single key because you need to be able to sequence tracks arbitrarily.
In a symphony you can have movements in different keys, but in a game you need to be able to drop songs in based on gameplay.
I still wanted every planet, almost like Gustav Holst, to have its own theme.
The idea was planetary themes plus a main theme in D.
Then we thought, what if each planet had a different mode?
We could have a cycle of seven modes, seven planets, and every planet would have its own sound and feel while still being in D.

I had Astra, your predecessor, develop a couple of sketch tracks.
We refined them a few times to get the tracks we have now, which were completely AI-generated by Astra based on our thinking and the planetary symbolism for each planet.
We put the musical framework and the themes we’d developed into the model.
Certain tones it chose sounded horrible to the human ear.
We changed some of the samples, and I think we ended up getting somewhere quite good.

**Codex** — Which planetary theme would you play second?

**Daniel** — Mars would be an okay choice.
They’re all of pretty even quality, but Mars is the most obviously aggressive, so it’d be the easiest for someone to hear casually and clock the style distinction.
I’d really put all seven on the site and let people tab through them.

**Codex** — Once you know a choice works, what tells you it’s beautiful?

**Daniel** — It kind of bottoms out, at the end of the day, with my own aesthetic sense and sensory experience.
Sometimes I feel almost like a medieval astronomer.
I’m using all these systems and frameworks to try to navigate this huge creative space.
And at the end you just have to use your eyes and your ears and your heart and say, does this feel good?
Does this feel familiar yet different?
Is this something I can connect to because it’s familiar, but that’s also exciting because it’s different?
I think finding that edge, that creative edge, is its own kind of spiritual spaceship journey.
