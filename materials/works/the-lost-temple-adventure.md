# The Lost Temple Adventure

*2025 · Onchain game and in-person treasure hunt · Devconnect, Buenos Aires*

For a week in November 2025, flyers around the Devconnect conference in Buenos Aires asked, “Can you find the lost temple?”
Scanning one gave you a map: a sheet of parchment with a temple at the top and five waypoints below, arranged differently for every player.
Two waypoints were won by playing a game on your phone.
The other three were puzzles somewhere in the venue: a Super Nintendo beside a stack of *Nintendo Power* magazines, a pile of Pokémon cards next to a type chart, and a Jenga set you had to win at against someone nearby.
Each solution was a codeword, and each codeword drew another stretch of the route onto your map.
The map was yours to keep.

Friends of mine had been running treasure hunts at Ethereum conferences for years, and I had helped with a few.
When they decided to sit this conference out, I asked if I could run one through Cartridge, where I worked.
What I love about a hunt is what it does to a place.
Once there might be something to find anywhere, you start looking everywhere.
I first felt that as a student, on a Halloween night in San Francisco, running back-to-back with two friends through a citywide game of humans and zombies.
The organizers had supplied a few rules, and the city supplied everything else.

The map carried the other idea I wanted to try.
People put hours into games, and the only record of that time is a save file nobody looks at.
Where does that energy go?
Here the save file was the artwork.
A small program on the blockchain gave every player a different route, after Erick Calderon’s idea of a “1 of 1 of X,” a series in which every piece is one of a kind.
I wanted to find out whether someone might come to care about their map because of the afternoon they had spent filling it in.

The story was only a gesture: there is a lost temple, go and find it.
That was enough to get someone to scan a flyer.
The puzzles were harder than I intended.
I was sure anyone who saw a Super Nintendo, a Konami game, and a puzzle called *Cracking the Code* would think of the Konami Code.
Almost nobody did.
A friend had talked me into putting a help link in the game, and it saved the hunt.
Ten players wrote to me for hints, and three of the four who finished were among them.
I had spent years wondering how a hunt could run without staff, and this one ran because players could reach me.

In all, 106 people started a map, 26 solved at least one level, and four reached the temple.
The Super Nintendo disappeared twice, the second time for good.
The photographs I like best from that week are of people working out the puzzles together.
