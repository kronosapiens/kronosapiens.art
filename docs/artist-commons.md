# Artist Commons references for the portfolio

The course files are in `~/Dropbox/Documents/Art/Artist Commons/`.
The numbers below are filename prefixes and the page numbers are PDF pages.
To read a passage locally, for example the sequencing test in lecture 162, run `pdftotext -f 33 -l 33 -layout 162*.pdf -` from that folder.

## Portfolio selection and documentation

- **113 and 162, How to Build a Portfolio.** These are two editions of the same lecture.
  In 162, pp. 5–20 cover choosing for a particular viewer, finding recurring questions in recent work, and judging what an older piece contributes.
  Pages 22–35 cover the opening and closing works, pacing, and when to group a project or draw connections across projects.
- **162, pp. 36–38 and 43–62.** A website lets people enter and browse in their own order.
  Identify the work consistently and choose documentation for what it reveals about the whole piece, its surface or its setting.
  The size examples on p. 21 refer to submissions (113 counts images and 162 counts PDF pages).
  Neither edition sets a target number of works for a website.
- **Nathaniel Stern, Writing One Project for Different Readers.** In `Artist_Commons_02_Writing_for_Different_Readers_Workbook.docx`, see “Labels websites and catalogs” and “Website strategy.”
  Start with one or two audiences and what each needs from the site, then decide how much prominence to give selected work, the archive and contact information.
  The companion slides (169, pp. 25, 29–31) distinguish encountering a work in a gallery from deciding, at a distance, to seek it out.

## Artist statements

- **133, How to Write About Your Art, Part 1, pp. 6–14 and 20–23.** Give someone a reason to look again.
  Describe something they could see or experience, connect that detail to a possible meaning, and show why it might matter to someone beyond the artist.
  Introduce the purpose before the technical process.
- **137, Part 2, pp. 5 and 12–19.** Find the interpretation the artist can stand behind.
  Trace it to a choice of form or an event in the work and leave room for someone else to see more.
  When a draft could describe many artists, ask which decision in this particular piece would surprise a friend and why it mattered.
- **140, Part 3, pp. 8–15.** When revising, try starting from the sentence that best expresses the artist’s thought.
  Read the passage aloud for rhythm, and ask a reader to mark where their attention drifted.
  Use that spot to decide what to move, make concrete or cut.
