# Artist Commons notes for the portfolio

These notes paraphrase the Artist Commons workshops on building a portfolio and writing an artist statement.
The examples come from Daniel’s work.

## Portfolio selection and documentation

### Make a selection

Pick a reader and a decision they could make after a short visit.
A curator considering a game for an exhibition needs evidence of what happens in the room.
A teacher choosing a work to discuss needs an example students can inspect and return to.
Those readers might enter through the same image and need different things from the work page.

Gather candidate works before settling on the homepage order.
Look across the group for a question, method or visual choice that recurs in the work Daniel makes now.
Try removing one piece and naming what disappears with it.
An older work earns its place when it reveals part of the practice that the recent work alone would leave out.
For example, *Endless Knot* gives a viewer a way to see what changed by the time Daniel painted *Identity Matrix*.
In the first mural he worked out how to fit a borrowed pattern to a wall.
In the second he devised a rule that gives every figure its turn in every pairing of colors.

### Sequence the encounter

Choose an opening work a visitor can grasp on its own.
Keep images of one project together when they build an understanding of how it works.
Interleave projects when the juxtaposition makes a specific detail easier to see in both.
Try the sequence with the captions covered to see whether the relationship survives.
Choose a closing work that leaves the viewer with a sense of where the practice is heading.

A submission PDF can assume a reading order and a page budget.
On a website, someone can land halfway through the selection, so give each work enough context to stand on its own and a useful route onward.
If the goal is an exhibition inquiry, follow that route as far as contacting the artist.
If the site also serves as an archive, check that a returning reader can find a particular work by title.
The course’s suggested submission sizes apply to PDFs and image sets.

### Choose documentation

A full view lets someone judge the composition.
A detail can show how a stencil edge meets the wall, while a wider view can establish how large the mural feels beside a doorway.
Compare two candidate images by asking what each lets the visitor see for the first time.
For physical work, check color, lighting and perspective against the work itself.
For a participatory piece, consider whether the missing evidence is a player’s action, a change on screen or what the player takes home.

Keep the title, date and medium legible alongside the images.
Use a caption for facts a viewer cannot establish by looking, such as the scale, site or a collaborator’s contribution.

## Artist statements

### Find a claim in the work

Start by describing a moment another person could observe.
Ask why the artist made that choice and what it changes for the person encountering the work.
In *Ronin’s Pact*, each completed trial adds a sword to the rack.
That detail gives a reader a way into Daniel’s idea that a save file can be an artwork someone keeps.
The technical account of how the picture is stored makes more sense once the reader knows why the player would want it.

If a draft names a theme that could fit dozens of artists, ask Daniel which decision in this piece he would describe to a friend.
Listen for the detail that matters to him and check it against the work.
When a reference to another artist helps, say what it helps the reader see.
Leave room for readers to interpret what they find.

### Revise with a reader

Read the statement beside one of the works it names.
Ask the reader to point out something they noticed on a second look and to mark the sentence where their attention wandered.
If the most convincing sentence is buried, try moving it earlier and read both versions aloud.
Keep the version that brings the reader to the work sooner, respecting Daniel’s chosen opening and voice.
